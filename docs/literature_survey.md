# Literature Survey — LASA-RxGuard

20 works across 6 themes, 1966–2024. Every citation was checked against a publisher or index record on 15 September 2026; details those records did not confirm (such as article numbers) are omitted rather than guessed.

## Research gap

- **Handwriting recognition** [1]–[8] reads text well, but its benchmarks carry no drug vocabulary and it never asks whether a correct-looking read is dangerous.
- **Prescription recognition** [9]–[11] brings in the medical vocabulary, but stops at transcription: the output is a word, not a risk judgement.
- **Drug-name confusion** [12]–[15] measures exactly that danger, but on typed names at approval or labelling time — never on an uncertain reading at the counter.

**Among the works surveyed here, none couples recognition uncertainty with a look-alike / sound-alike risk check at the point of dispensing. LASA-RxGuard sits in that gap.**

## 1. Handwriting recognition architectures

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [1] | **Graves et al., 2006** (ICML) — *Connectionist Temporal Classification: Labelling unsegmented sequence data with recurrent neural networks* | CTC loss lets a recurrent network learn from unsegmented input–label pairs by summing over every possible alignment. | Removes the need for pre-segmented training data; demonstrated on speech (TIMIT). | Alignment only exists when the input has enough frames per label — a hard constraint the method does not relax. | Training loss for our CRNN. That frame constraint caused our worst silent bug: 47.8% of samples were infeasible until input width was fixed. |
| [2] | **Shi, Bai & Yao, 2017** (IEEE TPAMI) — *An end-to-end trainable neural network for image-based sequence recognition and its application to scene text recognition* | CRNN: convolutional feature extraction, BiLSTM sequence modelling and CTC transcription in one trainable network. | Reads arbitrary-length sequences without character segmentation, in a compact model. | Evaluated on scene text rather than handwriting, with no domain vocabulary. | Architecture basis for our from-scratch recogniser (8.4M parameters). |
| [3] | **Puigcerver, 2017** (ICDAR) — *Are multidimensional recurrent layers really necessary for handwritten text recognition?* | Compares a CNN with 1-D BLSTM layers against multidimensional LSTM for handwritten text. | The simpler CNN-BLSTM outperforms MDLSTM models at lower computational cost. | General line-level handwriting; no medical lexicon. | Justifies a 1-D BiLSTM head over MDLSTM — which matters under a counter-device latency budget. |
| [4] | **Li et al., 2023** (AAAI) — *TrOCR: Transformer-based optical character recognition with pre-trained models* | Pre-trained image Transformer encoder with a text Transformer decoder generating wordpieces. | State of the art on printed, handwritten and scene text recognition at publication. | Large and autoregressive: expensive to fine-tune, and hard to export for edge devices. | Our comparison arm. It did not converge on the compute available and is reported as a failed run, not as a result. |

## 2. Datasets and synthetic training data

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [5] | **Marti & Bunke, 2002** (IJDAR) — *The IAM-database: An English sentence database for offline handwriting recognition* | 1,066 forms from about 400 writers: 82,227 word instances over a 10,841-word vocabulary. | Became the standard benchmark for offline handwriting recognition. | General English prose — it contains essentially no medication names. | Our real-handwriting source. Its vocabulary gap is exactly why we generated a drug-name corpus. |
| [6] | **Simard, Steinkraus & Platt, 2003** (ICDAR) — *Best practices for convolutional neural networks applied to visual document analysis* | Expands the training set with elastic distortions built from smoothed random displacement fields. | Enlarging training data through distortion was the single most important practice. | Handwritten digits; no word-level or cursive variation. | Elastic distortion is a core stage of our image degradation pipeline. |
| [7] | **Jaderberg et al., 2014** (NIPS DL Workshop) — *Synthetic data and artificial neural networks for natural scene text recognition* | A synthetic text rendering engine; models trained with no human-labelled data at all. | Realistic synthetic data can substitute for real labelled data. | Printed scene text, not handwriting. | Precedent for training on generated images. We extend it to handwriting fonts with per-glyph jitter and a legibility guard. |
| [8] | **Fogel et al., 2020** (CVPR) — *ScrabbleGAN: Semi-supervised varying length handwritten text generation* | A generative adversarial network that produces handwritten word images of arbitrary length and style. | Generated handwriting improves recognition when real labelled data is scarce. | Requires training a GAN, which adds cost and makes label fidelity harder to guarantee. | Considered and deferred in favour of reproducible fonts plus augmentation. The strongest next step for the style gap we measured. |

## 3. Prescription handwriting recognition

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [9] | **Tabassum et al., 2022** (Scientific Reports) — *An online cursive handwritten medical words recognition system for busy doctors in developing countries for ensuring efficient healthcare service delivery* | Bidirectional LSTM on online (stylus) handwriting, trained on a Handwritten Medical Term Corpus of 17,431 samples of 480 terms. | 93.0% average accuracy with data augmentation. | Captures pen movement rather than photographed paper, and recognises words without assessing confusion risk. | Closest domain precedent; supports a BiLSTM for medical vocabulary. We target offline images and add the risk stage. |
| [10] | **Pavithiran G. et al., 2022** (arXiv) — *Doctor's handwritten prescription recognition system in multi-language using deep learning* | CNN, RNN and LSTM models that transcribe prescriptions written in several languages. | Deep sequence models can turn illegible prescriptions into readable text. | Aimed at transcription; look-alike confusion risk is outside its stated scope. | Relevant to multi-language Indian prescriptions — listed as a future extension. |
| [11] | **Ali et al., 2024** (arXiv) — *Leveraging deep learning with multi-head attention for accurate extraction of medicine from handwritten prescriptions* | Mask R-CNN locates medicine names on the page; TrOCR with multi-head attention reads them. | A two-stage detect-then-read pipeline for whole prescription images. | Transformer-based and heavy to deploy; stops at extraction rather than confusion risk. | Validates detect-then-read for full chits. Our word segmentation is a lighter version of that first stage. |

## 4. Drug-name confusion (LASA)

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [12] | **Lambert et al., 1999** (Medical Care) — *Similarity as a risk factor in drug-name confusion errors: The look-alike (orthographic) and sound-alike (phonetic) model* | Models confusion risk as a function of orthographic and phonetic similarity between drug names. | Look-alike and sound-alike similarity act as distinct risk factors for confusion errors. | Works on typed names; there is no handwriting and no recognition uncertainty. | Foundation for fusing spelling and sound similarity instead of relying on either alone. |
| [13] | **Kondrak & Dorr, 2006** (Artif. Intell. Med.) — *Automatic identification of confusable drug names* | Combines orthographic and phonetic similarity measures to rank how confusable two names are. | Became the basis of an FDA system that screens proposed drug names for confusability. | Screens names at approval time, not readings taken at the dispensing counter. | Direct precedent for our fusion. Our ablation reaches the same conclusion: no single measure is enough. |
| [14] | **Filik et al., 2004** (Soc. Sci. Med.) — *Drug name confusion: Evaluating the effectiveness of capital ("Tall Man") letters using eye movement data* | Eye-tracking experiment comparing Tall Man capitalisation with ordinary lowercase drug names. | Tall Man lettering improved how accurately similar names were perceived. | Printed text only. A handwritten prescription has no controlled capitalisation. | Every alert shows the tall-man form, restoring the mitigation that handwriting strips away. |
| [15] | **WHO, 2007** (Patient Safety Solutions) — *Look-alike, sound-alike medication names* | International guidance and recommended safeguards from the WHO Collaborating Centre for Patient Safety Solutions. | Confusing drug names are one of the most common causes of medication error worldwide. | Guidance rather than tooling — it recommends safeguards but supplies no automated check. | Establishes the clinical significance and global scope of the problem we address. |

## 5. String and phonetic similarity

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [16] | **Levenshtein, 1966** (Soviet Phys. Doklady) — *Binary codes capable of correcting deletions, insertions, and reversals* | Defines edit distance as the minimum number of insertions, deletions and substitutions. | Shows that this edit distance is a metric. | Treats every substitution as equal, although some letters look far more alike in handwriting. | Underlies our edit signal (Damerau variant) and the character error rate metric itself. |
| [17] | **Winkler, 1990** (ASA Proceedings) — *String comparator metrics and enhanced decision rules in the Fellegi-Sunter model of record linkage* | Extends the Jaro string comparator to reward agreement on the leading characters. | Improved matching of names with typographical variation in 1990 U.S. Census record linkage. | Designed for typing errors in records, not for handwriting. | Jaro-Winkler leads our edit signal, since handwriting tends to degrade toward the end of a word. |
| [18] | **Philips, 2000** (C/C++ Users Journal) — *The double metaphone search algorithm* | Phonetic encoding that returns both a primary and a secondary code for ambiguous pronunciations. | Handles spelling variants that single-code schemes such as Soundex collapse. | Designed around English surnames, not pharmaceutical nomenclature. | The highest-weighted phonetic code in our sound-alike signal. |

## 6. Clinical alerting and edge deployment

| Ref | Work | Method | Key finding | Limitation / gap | Use in LASA-RxGuard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [19] | **van der Sijs et al., 2006** (JAMIA) — *Overriding of drug safety alerts in computerized physician order entry* | Review of 17 studies of how clinicians respond to drug safety alerts. | Clinicians override drug safety alerts in 49% to 96% of cases. | Diagnoses override behaviour but does not set alert thresholds for new systems. | The reason we recalibrated: false alarms fell from 36% to 15.5% with no loss of recall. |
| [20] | **Jacob et al., 2018** (CVPR) — *Quantization and training of neural networks for efficient integer-arithmetic-only inference* | 8-bit integer-only inference, co-designed with quantization-aware training. | Efficient on integer-only hardware with accuracy preserved; the basis of TensorFlow Lite quantization. | Not evaluated on CTC sequence recognisers. | Basis for our INT8 size estimate (34 MB to about 8 MB), which still has to be re-measured on our model. |

## Data sources (not surveyed)

The ground truth and corpus come from reference datasets rather than papers: the ISMP List of Confused Drug Names, the FDA/ISMP tall-man lettering list, RxNorm (U.S. National Library of Medicine) and the National List of Essential Medicines (India, 2022). See the main README for how each is used.
